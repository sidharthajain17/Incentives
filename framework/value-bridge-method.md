# Value bridge method

How to get from the headline number in a policy to what the project actually receives. Run on every mandate, before any state comparison.

The headline is almost always a ceiling expressed as a percentage of eligible investment over a period. It is not a forecast. The job is to work out what survives.

---

## The bridge

Six steps, in this order. Each one is a haircut. Show the number at every step so the CFO can see where it went.

| Step | What it asks | Typical effect |
|---|---|---|
| 1. Headline | What does the policy say the ceiling is? | Starting point |
| 2. Eligible investment | How much of the project cost actually qualifies? | Often the largest single cut |
| 3. Instrument caps | What do the per-instrument and annual caps allow? | Spreads and truncates |
| 4. Absorption | Can the project generate enough base to absorb it? | Decisive for tax-linked benefits |
| 5. Timing | When does the cash actually arrive? | Cuts present value |
| 6. Certainty | What is the realistic probability of payment? | Cuts it again |

The answer is expressed two ways. As a percentage of **total project cost**, not of eligible investment, because that is the number a promoter has in mind. And as a rupee figure with its arrival profile.

---

## Step 2. Eligible investment

Build the bridge from project cost down.

```
Total project cost
  less  land, where not eligible
  less  pre-operative and preliminary expenses
  less  interest during construction
  less  working capital
  less  second-hand plant, where not eligible
  less  spend outside the investment window
  =     eligible investment
```

Check the policy wording on each line. Also check the test applied: paid for, acquired, capitalised, or installed and commissioned. These give different answers at the cut-off date.

For most manufacturing projects the eligible figure sits well below the project cost. Compute it, never assume a ratio.

---

## Step 3. Instrument caps

Three caps usually operate together and the tightest one governs.

The **overall cap**, a percentage of eligible investment across all instruments. The **per-instrument cap**, which limits any single benefit. The **annual cap**, which limits what can be drawn in a year.

The annual cap is the one most often missed. An entitlement of a given size with a low annual cap does not pay out over the policy tenure. It pays out over whatever period the cap allows, and anything left when the tenure ends is lost.

Always test: entitlement divided by annual cap, against the tenure. If the first number is larger than the tenure, part of the entitlement is dead on arrival.

---

## Step 4. Absorption

Only relevant for tax-linked instruments, and this is where large entitlements most often collapse.

A state goods and services tax reimbursement is paid against state tax the unit actually pays **in cash**, on **intra-state** sales. So test the project's own numbers:

- What share of sales is inside the state? Inter-state sales generate integrated tax, not state tax.
- What share is export? Exports generate none.
- What is the input tax credit position? Where credit is ample, cash payment is small, so the pool is small.
- Is the structure inverted, meaning inputs taxed higher than output? Then there may be refunds rather than cash payments.
- Are goods moved to branches or depots in other states before sale? That changes where the tax lands.

Compute the annual state tax pool from the project's own sales plan. Compare it against the annual entitlement. The lower of the two is what gets paid.

A project selling mostly outside the state can hold a large paper entitlement and receive very little.

---

## Step 5. Timing

Lay the cash out year by year. Note the start trigger, the claim cycle, and the observed lag between claim and payment in that state. Discount at the company's own cost of capital, not a notional rate.

---

## Step 6. Certainty

Apply a judgement factor, stated openly and tagged, based on:

- The state's disbursement record and pending arrears
- Whether the benefit sits in a budget line or is self-funding for the state
- Whether there is a written agreement with a stability clause
- Whether the policy has been amended mid-term before
- Whether sanction is at district, state committee or Cabinet level

State-tax-linked support is closer to self-funding for a state, since the state is returning part of what it collected. A capital subsidy is cash out of the budget. The first is generally more reliable than the second, but record the state's actual record rather than relying on the principle.

---

## Output

A single table the CFO can read in one minute.

| Line | Amount | Note |
|---|---|---|
| Headline entitlement | | % of eligible investment, policy reference |
| After eligible investment test | | What was excluded |
| After caps | | Which cap binds |
| After absorption | | Pool versus entitlement |
| Present value | | Discount rate used |
| Risk adjusted | | Factor and reasoning |
| **Effective yield** | | **As % of total project cost** |

Alongside it, one line on certainty of payment, because yield and certainty are two different questions and a project should never be chosen on yield alone.
