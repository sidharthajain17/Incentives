# Idea levers

The thinking moves run on every mandate, after the policies are decoded and the value bridge is built. Reading the policy is the starting point. These are the job.

Method. Go wide first, fifteen to twenty ideas without filtering. Then rank by rupee value, feasibility and risk, and keep the top five. Run each survivor through the four-chair test. Anything that survives and is reusable becomes a play in `plays/`.

---

## The levers

**Instrument fit.** Given how this project actually makes money, which instrument pays and which is decoration? A power-heavy project should be arguing about tariff and open access, not about a small capital subsidy.

**Cross-state transplant.** Which state does this particular thing better, and can the chosen state be asked to match it? A term that exists in a neighbouring policy is a far easier ask than something invented.

**Unit design.** One project or separate units and phases. Separate entities can fit size-class and new-unit tests better. One combined project can clear a mega threshold that unlocks a negotiated package. Both have to carry real commercial substance, and anti-splitting and related-party clauses have to be checked.

**Value chain move.** Does adding a step upstream or downstream move the product into a thrust category, or over a value-addition threshold, or into a central scheme that was otherwise out of reach?

**Anchor and ancillary.** Can the project bring its suppliers in alongside it? A cluster is worth more to a state than a single unit, and that is leverage in a negotiation.

**Stacking.** Which central scheme combines with which state top-up, and where is the choose-one rule. This is usually buried in a proviso and is worth finding before anything is committed.

**Timing.** Old policy or new. Transition windows. Whether to commence spend before or after a date. State budget cycles, since a claim filed into a fresh budget year behaves differently from one filed at the end of it.

**Policy silence.** Where the policy says nothing, there are three routes: read it favourably and build the evidence, ask for a written clarification, or take it into the customised package. Choose deliberately rather than by default.

**Non-cash value.** Land readiness, power infrastructure, approval speed, water allocation. For a project on a tight schedule these are often worth more than the subsidy.

**Certainty engineering.** Escrow, payment security, a stability clause, a dedicated budget line, staged commitments tied to the state's own milestones.

**Shape of the ask.** The same rupee value can be asked for in forms that cost the state very differently. Tax-linked support is close to self-funding for a state. Capital subsidy is cash out of its budget. Ask in the form the state can actually say yes to.

**Framing in the state's terms.** Jobs created, tax generated, anchor effect, district development, export earnings. The case is made in the state's language, not the investor's.

**Advocacy.** Where no policy fits, the move may be to propose one. Works best positioned as an industry-wide case rather than a single-company request.

**Red team.** What would the state committee object to? Where is the clawback risk in three years? What would an auditor flag? Run this before delivering, not after.

---

## The four-chair test

Every surviving idea is tested from four seats, and the answer recorded.

| Chair | Question |
|---|---|
| Chief financial officer | Will the money actually come, and when? |
| State committee | Will they approve this, and what will they object to? |
| Auditor | Where is the clawback risk? |
| Lender | Will they count this in the project financials? |

An idea that fails from any chair is either dropped or redesigned. It is not delivered with the failure unstated.

---

## Certainty

Every idea carries a tag of fact, interpretation, market view or evolving, and the evidence behind it. An idea resting on precedent names the precedent. An idea resting on a reading of silence says so.
